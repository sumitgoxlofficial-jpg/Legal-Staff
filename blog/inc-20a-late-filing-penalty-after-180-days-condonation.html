Missed the 180-Day INC-20A Commencement Deadline? Penalty & Condonation Guide

Missing the 180-day INC-20A window does not end the company, but it starts a penalty clock and can invite strike-off. Here is how to file late, respond to the ROC and limit the exposure.

Executive summary

Every company incorporated with share capital after the Companies (Amendment) Ordinance, 2019 must file e-form INC-20A, the declaration for commencement of business, within 180 days of incorporation. Section 10A of the Companies Act, 2013 says the company cannot commence business or exercise borrowing powers until a director files this declaration confirming that every subscriber has paid for the shares agreed to be taken. If the window is missed, the company does not cease to exist, but it is in continuing default.

The consequences are a fixed penalty on the company, a per-day penalty on every officer in default, and a power for the Registrar of Companies (ROC) to start strike-off proceedings under section 248 if it believes the company is not carrying on business. The practical route out is usually the same: collect proof that subscription money was actually received, file INC-20A late with the additional fee the portal computes, and deal with the penalty through the adjudication process under section 454 rather than ignoring it.

One caution before you rely on any "condonation" advice you read online: past amnesty and condonation-of-delay schemes run by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA) were time-bound. Do not assume any scheme is open today. Check the MCA website for current general circulars, or ask a practising professional to confirm, before choosing a route.

How to regularise a late INC-20A, step by step

  1. Confirm the actual position on the MCA portal

    Check the company master data on the MCA V3 portal: the incorporation date, the status (Active, or "Under process of striking off"), and whether any INC-20A is already on record. Count the days from incorporation. If the status already shows strike-off action, skip to the strike-off step below, because the order of work changes.

  2. Prove that subscribers actually paid

    INC-20A is a declaration that every subscriber to the memorandum has paid the value of the shares agreed to be taken. Get the bank statement showing each subscriber's money credited to the company's current account. If subscribers never paid, they must pay now, and the declaration must be truthful as on the date of filing. A false declaration is a far bigger problem than a late one.

  3. Check registered office verification and sectoral approvals

    Section 10A also requires that the company has filed verification of its registered office under section 12(2). For most companies incorporated through SPICe+ this was done at incorporation, but check. If the business needs registration or approval from a sectoral regulator (for example the RBI for an NBFC), the INC-20A asks for it, so keep it ready.

  4. Prepare and certify the e-form

    A director signs INC-20A with a valid DSC, and the form is certified by a practising Chartered Accountant, Company Secretary or Cost Accountant. Attach the bank statement as proof of subscription. Make sure the signing director's DIN is active and DIR-3 KYC is up to date, or the form will not go through.

  5. Pay the normal fee plus additional fee

    When INC-20A is filed after the due date, the MCA portal levies an additional fee under the Companies (Registration Offices and Fees) Rules, 2014, which rises with the length of delay. Use the fee shown on the portal at the time of filing as the authoritative figure rather than a number quoted in an article. Paying the additional fee does not settle the section 10A penalty; that is a separate liability.

  6. Handle the penalty through adjudication under section 454

    Section 10A is a civil penalty provision, so it is dealt with by the ROC acting as adjudicating officer under section 454 and the Companies (Adjudication of Penalties) Rules, 2014. Typically the ROC issues a show-cause notice; the company and officers reply, and an order is passed. Some companies choose to approach the adjudicating officer themselves after filing, which shows good faith. Keep the reply factual: dates, reasons, proof of payment, and the date the default was made good.

  7. If strike-off has started, respond within the notice period

    If the ROC has issued a notice under section 248 (in Form STK-1 or the corresponding notice) or the company appears in a public notice of proposed strike-off, file a reply with evidence that the company is carrying on business: bank statements, invoices, GST returns, contracts. If the name has already been struck off, revival is generally through an application to the National Company Law Tribunal under section 252, which is slower and costlier. Act before that stage.

  8. Put the rest of year-1 compliance on a calendar

    A late INC-20A often signals other gaps: first auditor appointment, share certificates, statutory registers, DIR-3 KYC. Fix them together so the adjudication reply does not reveal new defaults. Our ROC compliance calendar lists the next dates for your entity.

Penalty versus additional fee: two different things

Founders often assume that the late fee paid on the portal closes the matter. It does not. The additional fee is a filing charge for accepting a form after its due date. The section 10A penalty is a liability imposed by an adjudication order. You can pay the additional fee today and still receive a show-cause notice months later.

The officer-in-default penalty is personal to each director who was in default. It accrues per day while the default continues, which is why filing as early as possible matters even after the deadline: it stops the clock. For small companies and start-ups, section 446B, inserted by the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2020, caps penalties at one-half of the specified amount subject to an upper limit. Whether your company qualifies on the relevant date is a question of fact, so confirm it with the adviser preparing your reply.

What about condonation schemes?

Over the years the MCA has run several one-time schemes that allowed companies to file overdue documents with reduced or waived additional fees, and some offered immunity from certain proceedings. Each had a fixed window and conditions, and each ended. Because these schemes are announced through general circulars and then lapse, we do not describe any scheme as currently available.

If you hear that a scheme is open, check the MCA website's circulars section for the current text, read the conditions (many exclude companies already under adjudication or strike-off), and note the closing date. Otherwise, plan on the standard route: late filing with additional fee plus adjudication.

  • Search the MCA circulars page for the scheme name and check the closing date.
  • Confirm whether INC-20A is a covered form under that scheme.
  • Check whether pending adjudication or strike-off notices exclude you.
  • Keep the scheme circular and the filing receipt in the company records.

Common pitfalls

Filing a declaration that is not true

If subscription money was never received, do not file INC-20A until it is. A false declaration can attract consequences far heavier than the section 10A penalty.

Using a director with an inactive DIN

If the signing director missed DIR-3 KYC, the DIN may be deactivated and the form will be rejected. Fix the KYC first.

Ignoring a show-cause notice

An unanswered adjudication notice usually leads to an order on the record as it stands. Reply in time, with documents.

Borrowing or trading before filing

Section 10A bars commencing business and exercising borrowing powers until the declaration is filed. Signing loans or large contracts in the gap adds to the problem.

Treating strike-off as a formality

Once the name is struck off, bank accounts can be frozen and revival needs an NCLT order. Respond to any strike-off notice immediately.

Not fixing the other year-1 defaults

Late INC-20A is often accompanied by a missed first-auditor appointment or unissued share certificates. Clean them up together.

Frequently asked questions

Can INC-20A be filed after 180 days?

In practice the MCA portal has accepted INC-20A after 180 days with an additional fee, provided the company is still active. Filing late does not remove the section 10A penalty, which is imposed separately through adjudication. If the company is already under strike-off, the reply to the strike-off notice comes first.

What is the penalty for not filing INC-20A?

Under section 10A(2), the company is liable to a penalty of ₹50,000 and every officer in default to ₹1,000 for each day the default continues, subject to a maximum of ₹1,00,000. Small companies, OPCs and start-up companies may get the lower penalty under section 446B; confirm whether it applies to you.

Will the ROC strike off my company for not filing INC-20A?

It can. Section 10A(3) allows the Registrar, where no declaration is filed within 180 days and it has reasonable cause to believe the company is not carrying on business, to start removal of the name under section 248. Showing real business activity and filing the declaration are the best defence.

Is there a condonation scheme for INC-20A right now?

We do not assume so. Earlier MCA schemes that relaxed fees or offered immunity were time-bound and have closed. Check the MCA website for any current circular, read its conditions and closing date, or ask a practising professional before you rely on one.
