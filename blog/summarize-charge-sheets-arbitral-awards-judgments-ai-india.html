How to Summarise Charge Sheets, Arbitral Awards and Judgments with AI in India

A 400-page charge sheet, a 90-page award, a 60-page judgment. AI can give you a first map of each — if you know what to ask for and check what comes back.

Executive summary

Long documents arrive at the worst moments: a charge sheet just before a bail hearing, an arbitral award with a challenge period already running, a judgment the client wants explained by tomorrow. The first read is about orientation — what is in it, where, and what it means for the next step.

AI summaries are good at that first map, provided they work from the document itself and point to the page or paragraph behind each statement. They are not good at deciding what matters in your case, and they can omit or compress things that later turn out to be decisive.

This guide lists what to extract from each type of document, the deadlines that start running when you receive them, and how to use Legal Staff's summaries with paragraph references as a starting point rather than a substitute for reading.

A summarising routine for long documents

  1. Get a clean, complete copy

    Make sure you have every volume and annexure. Charge sheets in particular are often supplied in parts; a summary of half the record is worse than none.

  2. Upload it to the case, not a public tool

    Keep the document in the case's own vault so the summary and any later questions stay within your firm's workspace.

  3. Ask for a structured summary

    Ask for the specific fields that matter for the document type (see the table below), each with a page or paragraph reference. Legal Staff's summaries cite the paragraphs they rely on.

  4. Check the references

    Open each referenced page and confirm the summary says what the document says. Pay particular attention to dates, amounts, names and anything that sounds like a finding.

  5. Diary every deadline the document triggers

    A judgment starts the appeal clock; an award starts the challenge clock; a charge sheet moves the case to the next stage. Put the last date on the calendar the day the document is received.

  6. Write your own note on top

    Use the summary to write the note that actually matters: strengths, weaknesses, what to argue and what to file next. That part is yours.

Charge sheets: what a useful summary looks like

A charge sheet summary for the defence should map the prosecution's case: who is said to have done what, on which evidence. The witness list is the backbone — for each witness, what they are cited to prove. The documents and seizure memos, forensic and medical reports, and any statements recorded before a magistrate follow.

Equally important is what is missing: reports awaited, witnesses not examined, documents referred to but not supplied. Gaps in the supplied copy should be raised at the stage of supply of documents to the accused. A summary that only restates what is present will miss this; ask for gaps explicitly and check them yourself.

Arbitral awards: the section 34 clock

Under section 34(3) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, an application to set aside an award must be made within three months from the date the party received the award or, if a request was made under section 33 (correction or interpretation), from the date that request was disposed of. The court may allow a further period of up to 30 days if it is satisfied the applicant was prevented by sufficient cause — but not thereafter.

That outer limit is strict, so the date of receipt matters. Record when and how the signed award was delivered, compute both dates, and put the three-month date on the calendar as the working deadline. The grounds for setting aside are limited and should be read in the current text of section 34; a summary of the award's reasoning helps identify them but is not a substitute for that analysis.

Judgments: summary versus headnote

A good summary of a judgment separates the facts, the issues, the answer on each issue with reasons, and the operative directions. It should not invent a ratio that the court did not state. When you rely on a judgment in court, cite paragraphs from the judgment itself, not from a summary.

Common pitfalls

Summarising an incomplete record

Volumes and annexures missing from the upload will be missing from the summary. Check completeness first.

Missing the date of receipt

For awards and judgments, limitation often runs from receipt or from the date of the decision. Record it the day the document arrives.

Relying on the summary's framing

A summary reflects what the document stresses, not what helps your client. Read the parts that matter for your argument.

Uncited statements

If a statement in a summary has no page or paragraph reference, treat it as unverified until you find it.

Sharing documents outside the workspace

Charge sheets and awards contain personal and commercial information. Keep them in the case vault rather than consumer tools.

Frequently asked questions

What is the time limit to challenge an arbitral award under section 34?

Three months from receipt of the award, or from disposal of a section 33 request, with a further period of up to 30 days if the court is satisfied there was sufficient cause, but not thereafter. Check the current text of section 34 and compute from the actual date of receipt.

Can AI summarise a charge sheet accurately?

It can give a useful first map — sections, roles, witnesses and documents — with page references. It can miss nuance and gaps, so check each reference and read the parts that matter for the defence.

Should I cite an AI summary in court?

No. Cite the judgment or document itself, with its paragraph numbers. Summaries are for your preparation.
