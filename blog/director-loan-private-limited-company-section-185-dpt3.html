Section 185 & Director Loans: Avoiding ROC Notices & Filing DPT-3 Properly

Money flowing from the company to a director and money flowing from a director to the company are treated very differently. Get the direction right, keep the declaration, and file DPT-3 on time.

Executive summary

In early-stage companies, money moves between founders and the company all the time: a director pays a vendor from a personal card, the company advances salary, a founder tops up the bank account before payroll. The Companies Act, 2013 treats these flows very differently depending on direction. Loans by the company to its directors are restricted by section 185. Loans by directors to the company are, in principle, deposits under section 73 unless they fall within an exemption in the Companies (Acceptance of Deposits) Rules, 2014.

For a private company, the most useful exemption is rule 2(1)(c)(viii): money received from a person who, at the time of receipt, was a director, or a relative of a director, is not a deposit if that person gives the company a written declaration that the amount is not being given out of funds acquired by borrowing or accepting loans or deposits from others, and the company discloses the details in the Board's report.

Both sides feed into Form DPT-3, the annual return of deposits and of particulars of transactions not treated as deposits, due by 30 June with figures as on 31 March. Many ROC notices in this area come from mismatches between the balance sheet, the DPT-3 and the Board's report, so the cure is consistent paperwork rather than complex law.

Getting director loans right, step by step

  1. Map every balance with a director

    From the ledger, list every amount owed by a director to the company and every amount owed by the company to a director or their relative, with dates and nature: loan, advance against salary, reimbursement due, or unpaid remuneration. Reimbursements and remuneration are not loans; mixing them up is the source of many problems.

  2. Test loans to directors against section 185

    Section 185(1) prohibits a company from advancing a loan, including a loan represented by a book debt, to its directors or directors of its holding company, their partners or relatives, and certain firms, or giving a guarantee or security for their loans. Section 185(2) allows loans to other persons in whom a director is interested only with a special resolution and use of the money for the borrower's principal business. Check the section 185(3) exceptions, such as loans to a managing or whole-time director under a scheme for all employees or approved by special resolution.

  3. Check the private company exemption

    A 2015 notification exempts certain private companies from section 185: broadly, those in whose share capital no other body corporate has invested, whose borrowings from banks, financial institutions or bodies corporate are less than twice the paid-up capital or ₹50 crore (whichever is lower), and which have no subsisting default in repaying those borrowings at the time of the transaction. All conditions must be met on the date of the loan. Once a corporate investor comes in, the exemption falls away.

  4. Take the declaration for loans from directors

    For each amount received from a director or a director's relative, obtain a written declaration at the time the money is given, stating that it is not out of borrowed funds. A declaration signed months later is weak evidence. Record the receipt in board minutes and keep the bank trail; avoid cash, because the Income-tax rules on cash loans apply separately.

  5. Disclose in the Board's report

    The rule 2(1)(c)(viii) exemption also requires the company to disclose the details of money accepted from directors in the Board's report. Make sure the amount matches the balance sheet and the DPT-3.

  6. File DPT-3 by 30 June

    Under rule 16 of the Deposit Rules, every company other than a government company files DPT-3 by 30 June, showing outstanding deposits and amounts not treated as deposits as on 31 March. Where the return covers deposits, it is audited by the company's auditor; check the current form instructions for what must be attached for exempted amounts only.

  7. Unwind any non-compliant loan

    If a loan to a director does not fit an exemption, take advice before the year-end. Recovering it, converting it into a proper arrangement, or regularising it with the correct approvals is better than leaving it for the auditor to report. Section 185 contraventions are punishable offences, not just filing defaults.

Why ROC notices arise in this area

Notices usually follow a mismatch rather than a deliberate breach. Typical triggers are a balance sheet showing "loans from directors" with nothing reported in DPT-3, a DPT-3 that reports exempt amounts but a Board's report that is silent, or "loans and advances to related parties" in the financial statements for a company that does not meet the private company exemption.

The auditor's report under the Companies (Auditor's Report) Order also asks about compliance with sections 185 and 186 for companies to which CARO applies, so a weak position tends to surface at audit. Fixing records before the audit is cheaper than answering a notice after it.

Tax and other angles to keep in view

The Companies Act is only one lens. Under the income-tax law, a loan or advance by a closely held company to a shareholder with substantial voting power (or to a concern in which they are substantially interested) can be taxed as deemed dividend to the extent of accumulated profits. The Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the 1961 Act from 1 April 2026, with provisions renumbered; check the current provision with your tax adviser.

Cash loans of significant amounts are separately restricted by the income-tax rules on acceptance and repayment of loans other than by banking channels. Use bank transfers for every director transaction.

  • Keep a director-wise ledger with nature of each entry.
  • Store declarations with the board minutes that note the receipt.
  • Reconcile ledger, balance sheet, DPT-3 and Board's report every year.
  • Re-test the private company exemption after every funding round.

Common pitfalls

Calling a director advance a "reimbursement"

If money goes to a director before expenses are incurred, it may be a loan. Use an expense policy and settle claims promptly.

Losing the exemption after funding

A corporate investor in the share capital ends the private company exemption from section 185 for future transactions.

Backdated declarations

The declaration is meant to be given at the time the money is given. Prepare a standard format and sign it with each transfer.

Skipping DPT-3 because there are no deposits

DPT-3 also reports amounts not treated as deposits. If you have director loans or other exempt amounts, a return is generally due.

Relatives' loans in a public company

The relative limb of rule 2(1)(c)(viii) applies to private companies. Public companies should check the exemption carefully.

Frequently asked questions

Can a private limited company give a loan to its director?

Only if the transaction falls outside section 185 or within an exception. Qualifying private companies have a conditional exemption under a 2015 notification, and section 185(3) has exceptions such as loans to a managing or whole-time director under an employee scheme or one approved by special resolution. Test every condition on the date of the loan.

Is a loan from a director a deposit?

Not if it falls within rule 2(1)(c)(viii) of the Deposit Rules: the director (or, for a private company, a relative of a director) gives a written declaration that the money is not from borrowed funds, and the company discloses the details in the Board's report. Otherwise it may be a deposit under section 73.

What is the due date for DPT-3?

DPT-3 is due by 30 June each year, reporting outstanding deposits and amounts not treated as deposits as on 31 March. Late filing attracts additional fees, and contraventions of the Deposit Rules can lead to penalties.

Does an OPC or small company need to file DPT-3?

Rule 16 applies to every company other than a government company, so small companies and OPCs are not exempt as a class. If you have deposits or exempted amounts such as director loans to report, plan to file.
