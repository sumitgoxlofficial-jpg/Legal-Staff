Per-Hearing, Retainer and Fixed-Fee Billing for Indian Advocates, with GST Basics

Per-hearing fees are simple to quote and easy to lose track of. Here is how to choose a fee model, how GST reverse charge works for legal services, and how to tie bills to the hearings that earned them.

Executive summary

Indian litigators bill in a few common ways: per appearance, per stage of a case, by monthly retainer, or by fixed fee for a defined piece of work. Each works when it is recorded against the hearings and tasks that justify it. Most revenue leakage in chambers comes not from the fee model but from appearances that never made it to an invoice.

GST adds a layer that often confuses clients. Legal services supplied by an individual advocate (including a senior advocate) or a firm of advocates to a business entity are generally taxed under the reverse charge mechanism, so the business client pays the GST directly. Legal services to individuals who are not business entities, and to business entities below the threshold set in the exemption notification, are exempt. The rate, exemptions and paperwork should be confirmed with a chartered accountant.

This guide covers the fee models, the Bar Council rule against contingency fees, the reverse charge basics and what to put on a fee bill, and how Legal Staff’s per-matter invoices link fees to hearings.

Setting up billing that matches your practice

  1. Choose the fee model per matter

    Agree the basis at the start and record it on the matter: per appearance (and whether a non-effective date is billed), per stage, monthly retainer, or a fixed fee for a defined scope. Mixed models are common, for example a fixed drafting fee plus per-appearance fees.

  2. Put the terms in writing

    A short fee letter avoids most disputes: the fee basis, what is excluded (court fees, clerkage, travel, certified copy charges), when bills are raised, payment terms and how adjournments are treated.

  3. Link every appearance to the matter

    Bill from the diary, not memory. Each hearing attended, with the advocate who appeared and the outcome, is the evidence for a per-appearance bill.

  4. Identify the client’s GST position

    Record whether the client is a business entity and its GSTIN. That decides whether reverse charge applies, and what the bill should say about GST.

  5. Raise bills on a fixed cycle

    Bill monthly or at the end of each stage. Long gaps make bills harder to justify and slower to pay.

  6. Reconcile payments and tax deducted

    Business clients often deduct tax at source on professional fees. Record the amount received and the tax deducted against each bill, and reconcile with your tax statements.

GST reverse charge, stated carefully

Under the GST reverse charge notification for services, legal services supplied by an individual advocate (including a senior advocate) or a firm of advocates to a business entity located in the taxable territory are taxed in the hands of the recipient. The business client pays the GST to the government; the advocate does not collect it on the bill.

Under the exemption notification, legal services by an advocate or firm of advocates to a person other than a business entity, and to a business entity whose aggregate turnover in the preceding financial year was up to the threshold for registration, are exempt. An advocate whose only supplies are under reverse charge is generally not required to register for GST. These are general statements: the exact entries, thresholds and the position for services between advocates should be confirmed with a chartered accountant.

Where reverse charge applies, many advocates note on the fee bill that GST is payable by the recipient under reverse charge. If the advocate is unregistered, the business client may need to issue its own self-invoice for its GST records.

  • Record the client’s name, address and GSTIN where it has one.
  • Show the matter, court and case number on each bill.
  • List each appearance or stage with its date and fee.
  • Show out-of-pocket expenses separately from professional fees.
  • Add a note on GST under reverse charge where it applies.

Handling adjournments, non-effective dates and disputes

Per-appearance billing raises one recurring question: is an adjourned or non-effective date billable? There is no single rule; it is a matter of agreement with the client. Many advocates bill a reduced fee for a date on which the matter was not reached, and a full fee where they appeared and the matter was heard. Whatever you choose, write it in the fee letter and apply it consistently.

When a client queries a bill, the strongest answer is a dated list of appearances with the outcome of each, drawn from the diary, together with copies of the orders. That is far easier to produce when every hearing and its outcome were recorded on the day. Keep fee correspondence on the matter too, so the history of what was agreed is not lost in an inbox.

Common pitfalls

Charging GST on a reverse charge bill

Where the business client pays GST under reverse charge, adding GST to the advocate’s bill is wrong. Note the reverse charge position instead.

Unbilled appearances

Appearances recorded only in a paper diary are easily left out of the month’s bills. Bill from the shared diary.

Vague retainer scope

A retainer that does not say what it covers leads to arguments about every extra application. Define the scope.

Success-linked fees

Fees contingent on the result are prohibited by the Bar Council of India Rules. Do not draft fee letters that make the fee depend on the outcome.

Mixing expenses with fees

Court fees, stamp duty and copying charges paid on the client’s behalf should be shown separately, with receipts.

Frequently asked questions

Do advocates charge GST to business clients?

Generally no. Legal services by an advocate or firm of advocates to a business entity are under reverse charge, so the business client pays the GST directly. Confirm the current notification and your own position with a chartered accountant.

Is GST payable on legal services to individuals?

Legal services by an advocate to an individual who is not a business entity are exempt from GST under the exemption notification.

Can an advocate charge a success fee?

No. The Bar Council of India Rules on professional conduct prohibit an advocate from stipulating a fee contingent on the results of litigation.
