Drafting Written Statements and Bail Applications Faster with an AI Workspace

Most of a written statement or bail application is structure. Templates and an AI workspace can handle the structure so you can spend your time on the parts that win or lose.

Executive summary

A written statement and a bail application are among the most frequently drafted documents in Indian practice, and both run on time pressure. The written statement has a statutory clock under Order VIII Rule 1 CPC; a bail application often has to be ready the same day the client calls.

Much of each document is predictable structure: cause title, preliminary objections, para-wise replies, verification; or the particulars of the FIR, custody, grounds and prayer. A drafting workspace can assemble that structure from templates and the case file, so the advocate's time goes into the substance.

This guide sets out the structure of both documents, the timelines that matter, and how Legal Staff's Drafting Studio and Case Brain fit in. The drafting — and the responsibility for every averment — remains the advocate's.

A faster drafting routine

  1. Start from the right template

    Pick the template for the document and forum — written statement, regular bail, anticipatory bail, reply. Legal Staff's Drafting Studio includes these among its templates, with cause title and parties filled from the case.

  2. Pull facts from the record, not from memory

    Ask the Case Brain to list the dates, allegations and documents from the plaint, FIR or charge sheet in the case file, with references to where each appears. Check the list against the documents before using it.

  3. Draft the parts that need judgment yourself

    Preliminary objections, the specific denials, the grounds for bail and the answer to the prosecution's likely objections are where the case is won. Dictate or type them; use AI suggestions only as a starting point.

  4. Check every averment against the documents

    Every date, amount and fact stated must match the record. A wrong date in a bail application or an admission by omission in a written statement can cost more than the time saved.

  5. Version, review and export

    Keep each version, let the senior review and comment, and export to DOCX or PDF in the format your court requires. Verification and affidavits must be signed by the right person after reading the final text.

  6. Diary the next deadline

    Once filed, put the next date and any linked deadline — rejoinder, reply to an IA, compliance with bail conditions — on the calendar.

Structure of a written statement

A written statement answers the plaint. The general rule is that allegations not specifically denied may be taken as admitted, so evasive or general denials are dangerous. A clear structure helps make sure nothing is left unanswered.

  • Cause title and description of the document.
  • Preliminary objections: jurisdiction, limitation, maintainability, non-joinder or mis-joinder, valuation and court fee, cause of action, suppression of facts.
  • Reply on merits: para-wise admission or specific denial of each paragraph of the plaint, with the defendant's version where it differs.
  • Additional facts and defences, including any set-off or counter-claim (which has its own requirements).
  • List of documents relied on and, in commercial suits, the prescribed disclosures.
  • Prayer, verification and, where required, the supporting affidavit or statement of truth.

Structure of a regular or anticipatory bail application

A bail application should let the court see quickly who the applicant is, what they are accused of, what has happened procedurally and why bail should be granted. Since 1 July 2024, provisions are cited under the BNSS and offences under the BNS for events after that date; older matters continue under the CrPC and IPC as applicable, so check which law applies to your case.

For anticipatory bail the focus is the apprehension of arrest and why custodial interrogation is unnecessary; for regular bail it is the custody period, the stage of investigation or trial and the conditions you can offer.

  • Cause title, court and the provision invoked (s.480, s.482 or s.483 BNSS as the case may be).
  • Particulars: FIR number, police station, sections alleged, date of arrest or apprehension, custody period.
  • Brief facts and the applicant's version.
  • Grounds: nature of the allegations, role attributed, stage of investigation or trial, antecedents, parity, health or other personal circumstances, willingness to abide by conditions.
  • Whether any earlier bail application was filed and its outcome — courts expect this to be disclosed.
  • Prayer, with suggested conditions, and the supporting affidavit.

Common pitfalls

General denials in a written statement

A blanket 'denied for want of knowledge' against every paragraph risks being treated as admission. Deny specifically and state your version.

Missing the Order VIII deadline

Diary the last date from the date of service, not the date of appearance, and treat 120 days in commercial suits as an outer limit you must not cross.

Not disclosing earlier bail applications

Failing to disclose an earlier rejected application can lead to dismissal and adverse comment. Record every bail application in the case history.

Wrong statute for the date of the offence

Whether the IPC/CrPC or the BNS/BNSS applies depends on dates and transitional provisions. Check before citing sections.

Letting AI text go out unread

AI suggestions can be fluent and wrong. Every averment must be read, checked against the record and owned by the advocate.

Unsigned or wrongly verified pleadings

Verification and affidavits must be by the right person, after reading the final version, in the form your court accepts.

Frequently asked questions

What is the time limit for filing a written statement?

Under Order VIII Rule 1 CPC, 30 days from service of summons, extendable for recorded reasons generally up to 90 days. In commercial suits the outer limit is 120 days, after which the right to file is forfeited. Check the bare Act and any court-specific rules.

Which BNSS sections deal with bail?

Section 480 BNSS covers bail in non-bailable offences before courts other than the High Court or Court of Session, section 482 covers anticipatory bail, and section 483 covers the special powers of the High Court and Court of Session. They broadly correspond to sections 437, 438 and 439 CrPC.

Can AI draft a bail application for me?

It can assemble the structure, fill particulars from your case file and suggest grounds. The final grounds, facts and disclosures are the advocate's responsibility and must be checked against the record before filing.

Does Legal Staff file documents in court?

No. Legal Staff helps you draft, version and export documents as DOCX or PDF. Filing — physical or through the court's e-filing system — is done by you.
