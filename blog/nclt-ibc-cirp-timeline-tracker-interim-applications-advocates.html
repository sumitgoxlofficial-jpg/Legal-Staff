NCLT and IBC Timelines for Advocates: Tracking CIRP, Interim Applications and NCLAT Appeals

A corporate insolvency matter generates many deadlines at once: the CIRP clock, claims, CoC meetings, interim applications and appeals. Here is the statutory frame and how to keep it on one board.

Executive summary

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 is built around time limits. Section 12 requires the corporate insolvency resolution process (CIRP) to be completed within 180 days of admission, extendable once by up to 90 days on a resolution of the committee of creditors passed by 66% of the voting share. A 2019 amendment added that the CIRP is to be completed within 330 days from the insolvency commencement date, including extensions and the time taken in legal proceedings.

The Supreme Court has read down the word mandatorily in that proviso: 330 days is the norm, but the Adjudicating Authority may allow more time in exceptional cases, for example where the delay is attributable to the tribunal or to litigation rather than the parties. Advocates should plan to 330 days and treat any further time as something to be justified, not assumed.

Around this core clock run the deadlines that create day-to-day work: the operational creditor’s demand notice, claims, interim applications under section 60(5), applications against avoidable transactions, and appeals to the NCLAT under section 61 within 30 days plus up to 15 more on sufficient cause. This guide sets them out and shows how to track them per matter.

Tracking an IBC matter from filing to appeal

  1. Classify the application

    Note whether the matter is a section 7 application by a financial creditor, a section 9 application by an operational creditor or a section 10 application by the corporate debtor itself. Each has different pre-filing steps, and the default threshold (currently one crore rupees) must be met.

  2. For section 9, diary the demand notice period

    An operational creditor first delivers a demand notice under section 8. The corporate debtor has 10 days from receipt to point out a pre-existing dispute or pay. The section 9 application can be filed only after that period.

  3. Record the insolvency commencement date

    The CIRP clock starts on the date of admission, which is the insolvency commencement date. Every later deadline, including 180 and 330 days, counts from it. Record the date from the order itself.

  4. Diarise day 180, the extension and day 330

    Create entries for day 180, a reminder well before it for the CoC resolution and extension application, day 270 if an extension is granted, and day 330 as the outer limit. Note any periods the tribunal has expressly excluded by order.

  5. Track the regulatory steps in the first weeks

    The IRP’s public announcement and the last date for claims (by regulation, 14 days from the IRP’s appointment, with later claims permitted only on conditions) generate early work for creditors. Check the current CIRP Regulations for each step.

  6. Log every interim application separately

    Each IA under section 60(5) or on avoidance transactions (sections 43, 45, 50 and 66) has its own filing date, listing dates and orders. Give each IA its own entry linked to the main company petition so orders do not get mixed up.

  7. Diarise appeal limitation on every order

    An appeal to the NCLAT lies within 30 days under section 61, with up to 15 further days on sufficient cause. An appeal from the NCLAT to the Supreme Court on a question of law lies within 45 days under section 62, extendable by 15 days. Diarise both ends for every adverse order.

Why IBC matters need a different diary

In an ordinary suit, the next date comes from the court. In a CIRP, many important dates come from the statute and the regulations, and they run whether or not the matter is listed. A resolution professional’s timeline, the CoC meeting schedule and the tribunal’s listings all move at once, and an advocate for a creditor, a resolution applicant or a suspended director may be watching several insolvencies at different stages.

NCLT benches list matters through their own cause lists, and adjournments and part-heard matters are common. Recording each order on the day it is uploaded, with the next date and any directions, matters more than in most forums because each IA can produce its own deadline.

  • One record per company petition, with the insolvency commencement date at the top.
  • Each IA as a linked sub-matter with its own dates and orders.
  • Statutory day counts (180, 270, 330) shown next to the court dates.
  • Appeal limitation entered for every order you may challenge.

Common pitfalls

Counting CIRP days from the filing date

The CIRP runs from the insolvency commencement date, which is the date of admission, not the date of filing the application.

Assuming exclusions without an order

Periods are excluded from the CIRP clock when the tribunal or an appellate court so directs. Record the order that excludes the period, not an informal assumption.

Treating 330 days as an automatic ceiling or as irrelevant

It is the norm under section 12, and further time needs to be justified to the Adjudicating Authority. Plan to it.

Missing the NCLAT appeal window

Section 61 allows 30 days and at most 15 more on sufficient cause. There is no further discretion beyond that period, so diarise it on the day the order is pronounced or uploaded.

Losing track of IAs

A CIRP can generate dozens of IAs. Without separate records, orders on one IA get filed against another and next dates are missed.

Frequently asked questions

How long can a CIRP last?

Section 12 provides 180 days from the insolvency commencement date, extendable once by up to 90 days, with an overall limit of 330 days including litigation time. The Supreme Court has held that the tribunal can allow more time in exceptional cases, so treat 330 days as the norm.

What is the limitation for an appeal to the NCLAT?

Thirty days under section 61 of the IBC, with up to 15 further days if the NCLAT is satisfied there was sufficient cause for the delay.

Does the Limitation Act apply to IBC applications?

Yes. Section 238A applies the Limitation Act, 1963, as far as may be, to proceedings and appeals before the NCLT, the NCLAT, the DRT and the DRAT.

Can Legal Staff track statutory IBC days as well as hearing dates?

Yes, as limitation entries that an advocate approves. You enter the insolvency commencement date and the rule; Legal Staff shows the calculation and sends reminders. It does not pull data from the NCLT portal automatically.

General information for advocates, not legal advice. Statutes, rules and court practice directions change; check the bare Act, the latest amendments and your court's rules before relying on any date or section.
