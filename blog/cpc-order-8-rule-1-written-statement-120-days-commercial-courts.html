Written Statement Deadlines under Order VIII Rule 1 CPC: 30, 90 and 120 Days Explained

Ordinary civil suits and commercial suits run on different written statement clocks. One is flexible in exceptional cases; the other ends in forfeiture at 120 days. Here is how to tell them apart and track them.

Executive summary

Order VIII Rule 1 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 requires a defendant to file a written statement within 30 days of service of summons. In an ordinary suit the court may allow more time for reasons recorded in writing, up to 90 days from service. In a commercial suit, the proviso added by the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 allows extension up to 120 days from service, on costs, and after that the defendant forfeits the right to file and the court cannot take the written statement on record.

The practical difference is large. The Supreme Court has treated the 90-day limit in ordinary suits as directory, so a late written statement can still be accepted in exceptional cases. It has treated the 120-day limit in commercial suits as mandatory. Missing day 120 in a commercial suit usually means defending without pleadings.

This guide sets out both regimes, the start date (service of summons), what a commercial written statement must contain, and how to put the 30th, 90th and 120th days in front of the whole team so that no one discovers the deadline at the next hearing.

How to track a written statement deadline

  1. Classify the suit first

    Check whether the suit is a commercial dispute under section 2(1)(c) of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 and of at least the specified value (currently three lakh rupees). If it is, the commercial proviso to Order VIII Rule 1 applies, along with the amended Order XI on documents. If unsure, plan to the commercial timeline.

  2. Fix the date of service of summons

    Time runs from service of summons. Record the actual date of service on the defendant, not the date the client mentions the papers or the date of first appearance. If the plaint copy or documents were not served with the summons, note it on the file and raise it at the first hearing.

  3. Diarise day 30 as the real deadline

    Treat the 30th day as the target in every suit. Extensions are discretionary, come with costs in commercial suits and need a reasoned application. A draft should exist by around day 20.

  4. Diarise the outer limit separately

    Add a second, clearly marked entry for day 90 in an ordinary suit or day 120 in a commercial suit. In a commercial suit, label it as the forfeiture date so that no one mistakes it for a soft deadline.

  5. If more time is needed, apply before day 30 runs out

    Move an application for extension with specific reasons (for example, documents held by a third party or a change of counsel) and offer to pay costs. In a commercial suit the court cannot extend beyond 120 days however good the reasons.

  6. Prepare the commercial-suit annexures together

    In commercial suits, the written statement must have a statement of truth, specific denials, and the defendant’s list of documents with the declaration required under Order XI. File any counter-claim along with it. Build these in parallel, not after the pleading is final.

  7. Record filing and diary the next step

    Once filed, record the filing date and diary number, upload the stamped copy to the case file and diary the next steps: replication if permitted, admission and denial of documents, and case management hearing in commercial suits.

Ordinary suits versus commercial suits

For ordinary suits, the 90-day ceiling was introduced to stop endless adjournments, but the Supreme Court has read it as directory: a court may accept a written statement after 90 days in exceptional situations, for reasons recorded and usually on costs. That flexibility is not a strategy. It needs a real explanation, and many courts are strict about it.

For commercial suits, the Commercial Courts Act added a proviso that ends with forfeiture: once 120 days from service of summons pass, the defendant loses the right to file and the court shall not allow the written statement to be taken on record. The Supreme Court has held this limit to be mandatory. Order VIII Rule 10, as it applies to commercial disputes, also bars the court from extending time beyond that period.

Before relying on any exception, read the current bare text of Order VIII Rule 1, the Commercial Courts Act and your High Court’s rules, and check recent decisions of your own High Court. The statutory text above is stable; the case law on edge situations (service by e-mail, non-service of documents, periods excluded by court orders) is not something to assume.

Common pitfalls

Counting from the first appearance

Time runs from service of summons, which is often earlier than the first date of appearance. Count from service.

Treating a commercial suit as ordinary

If the suit is a commercial dispute of the specified value, the 120-day forfeiture applies even if the case is listed before a court you think of as a civil court. Check the classification on day one.

Waiting for day 90 or 120 to ask for time

Courts are more receptive to an early, reasoned application than a last-day request. In commercial suits there is nothing to ask for after day 120.

Forgetting the documents

In commercial suits, the list of documents and the declarations under Order XI go with the written statement. Missing them creates a second problem even when the pleading is in time.

No statement of truth

A commercial written statement must be verified by a statement of truth in the prescribed form. Use a checklist so it is not left out in the rush to file.

Frequently asked questions

Is the 90-day limit for a written statement mandatory in ordinary suits?

The Supreme Court has treated it as directory for ordinary civil suits, so a court may accept a later written statement in exceptional cases for reasons recorded, often on costs. Do not plan around this; aim to file within 30 days.

What happens after 120 days in a commercial suit?

Under the proviso to Order VIII Rule 1 as amended by the Commercial Courts Act, the defendant forfeits the right to file the written statement and the court shall not allow it to be taken on record. The Supreme Court has held this limit to be mandatory.

From which date are the 30, 90 or 120 days counted?

From the date of service of summons on the defendant. Record the actual date of service and keep the proof, since the deadline depends on it.
